Single-item overall job satisfaction (Wanous tradition)

Licence verified: not yet verified · literature last reviewed: 2026-09-01 · grades last confirmed: 2026-09-02 · rubric v1.4

In plain English

Single-item overall job satisfaction (Wanous tradition) is a single item. What it claims to measure: Global (overall) job satisfaction, that is a person's summary evaluative judgement of their job as a whole, captured in one question rather than by aggregating facet items. Licence for an employer or vendor: no formal licence (verified 2026-07-12 (no instrument licence exists to verify; non-proprietary generic item)).

The published evidence is strongest for convergent and discriminant validity; weak for criterion validity against organisational outcomes (absence, turnover, performance). No published evidence was located for test-retest reliability and responsiveness to change: the registry searched and found none in the sweep to date, so if you need those properties evidenced, this instrument does not yet carry them. Criterion validity against a reference standard does not apply to this construct: no reference standard exists for it, so nothing is graded and nothing is missing.

Identity

Version: No single canonical form; a family of one-item global job-satisfaction questions (for example 'All in all, how satisfied are you with your job?') typically rated on a 5-point or 7-point scale. The tradition is anchored by the Wanous, Reichers and Hudy (1997) meta-analysis rather than by one authored item.

This is the best-evidenced property of the tradition. The anchoring meta-analysis pooled 28 correlations from 17 studies (N=7,682) and found a mean uncorrected correlation of .63 (SD .09) between single-item and scale measures of overall job satisfaction, rising to .67 corrected for reliability and to .72 (SD .05) for the strongest subset of scale measures (Wanous, Reichers & Hudy 1997). Extending the approach to facets, Nagy 2002 reported single-item facet items correlating .60 to .72 with the corresponding Job Descriptive Index facet scales (N=207). Dolbier et al. 2005 found a single global item correlated significantly with a multi-item job-satisfaction scale and with work, personality and health variables in 745 public-agency employees. Broader single-item methodological work supports the general pattern that single items track their multi-item counterparts closely enough for many purposes, though multi-item measures usually retain a modest edge (Bergkvist & Rossiter 2007; Fisher, Matthews & Gibbons 2016; Song et al. 2022). Discriminant evidence specific to job satisfaction is thinner than convergent evidence. The best subset in the meta-analysis comprised 8 correlations from 1,735 people, and the reliability-corrected overall mean of .67 carried an SD of .08 (Wanous, Reichers & Hudy 1997); the broader single-item programme drew on N=17 subject matter experts and N=1,634 survey respondents across two samples (Fisher, Matthews & Gibbons 2016) and on ecological momentary assessment data in which single- and multiple-item measures correlated .24 to .61 (Song et al. 2022); the advertising study abstract gives neither a sample size nor a correlation (Bergkvist & Rossiter 2007), and the public-agency study reports no coefficient values in its abstract (Dolbier et al. 2005).

There is no diagnostic or health reference standard for job satisfaction, which is a subjective attitude rather than a health state, so validation against a gold-standard diagnosis is not a meaningful target for this construct. Health correlations exist (for example associations with self-reported health in Dolbier et al. 2005) but health is a correlate, not a reference standard for the attitude itself.

Better supported here than for many attitude measures, though still limited. Nagy 2002 found single-item satisfaction accounted for incremental variance in self-reported job performance and turnover intentions beyond the Job Descriptive Index. Dolbier et al. 2005 showed a single item substantially predicted turnover intention by logistic regression in 745 employees. Evidence against hard organisational outcomes (recorded sickness absence, actual turnover, objective performance) rather than self-reported intentions is sparse for the single-item form specifically, so the grade is held down. Overall organisational-criterion evidence for the one-item measure is present but modest.

absence type: category-error the property does not apply to this construct

state: not_applicable · rubric v1.4 · literature as of 2026-09-01 · grade confirmed 2026-09-02

Not applicable. A single item has no inter-item covariance, so Cronbach alpha and omega cannot be computed. Reliability for a single item is instead estimated indirectly: Wanous, Reichers and Hudy used the correction-for-attenuation formula to back out a minimum reliability of roughly .45 to .69 depending on assumptions (Wanous, Reichers & Hudy 1997), a method set out more fully in Wanous & Reichers 1996. This estimated reliability (~0.67) is a corrected convergence estimate, not an internal-consistency coefficient, and must not be read as one.

No direct test-retest coefficient for a single-item overall job-satisfaction measure was located in the latest review pass. The tradition's reliability estimates are correction-for-attenuation estimates derived from cross-sectional convergence with multi-item scales (~0.67 corrected; minimum ~0.45 to .69), NOT test-retest stability coefficients (Wanous, Reichers & Hudy 1997; Wanous & Reichers 1996). The absence of a genuine temporal-stability coefficient for the single job-satisfaction item is itself the finding and a real gap. Fisher, Matthews & Gibbons 2016 reported 1-month and 3-month test-retest reliabilities for a battery of single organisational items, but those coefficients are not cleanly attributable to a specific canonical global job-satisfaction item and are treated as parent-battery evidence rather than a coefficient for this form.

Populations, languages and norms

Used very widely in occupational and organisational research across many countries and languages, but as a generic method rather than a standardised instrument with published norms. The anchoring evidence is predominantly North American (Wanous, Reichers & Hudy 1997; Nagy 2002; Dolbier et al. 2005), with a UK university-staff study by Oshagbemi 1999 reaching broadly similar conclusions about single versus multiple-item measures. There is no canonical norm table for a working-adult population in any country.

Criticisms and controversies

The single-item measurement debate is the core controversy of this whole track. Critics hold that single items cannot demonstrate internal reliability, cannot separate content from error, and lose the domain coverage of multi-item scales, so that multi-item measures should remain the default (Fisher, Matthews & Gibbons 2016; Song et al. 2022). Defenders argue that for concrete, singular and well-understood constructs single items can match multi-item scales in predictive validity while cutting respondent burden (Bergkvist & Rossiter 2007; Gardner et al. 1998), and structured guidance now exists on when a single item is defensible (Diamantopoulos et al. 2012). A specific hazard for job satisfaction is that the estimated ~0.67 reliability is a correction-for-attenuation artefact, not a measured reliability, and is easily over-interpreted. Global items also cannot localise dissatisfaction to a facet, limiting diagnostic and action-planning use.
